# BUS-620 Research Brief

## The Problem

Japan is the largest foreign holder of U.S. Treasury securities and maintains one of the world's largest sovereign debt markets. When the Federal Reserve and the Bank of Japan move interest rates in different directions, investors may shift capital between U.S. Treasury bonds and Japanese government bonds (JDGBs). These flows can influence exchange rates, bond yields, and financial stability in both countries.

Understanding the relationship is increasingly important because recent monetary policy changes have ended decades of ultra-low Japanese interest rates while U.S. rates remain elevated.

## Hypothesis

Despite the Bank of Japan's recent policy normalization, Japanese government bond yields and the USD/JPY exchange rate remain more sensitive to Federal Reserve policy actions than to Bank of Japan policy actions. Because Japanese institutions hold substantial U.S. Treasury securities and actively allocate capital across global fixed-income markets, changes in U.S. interest rates and Treasury yields continue to exert a stronger influence on Japanese financial conditions than domestic monetary policy.

## How I Would Know I Was Wrong

My hypothesis would be disproven if the evidence shows that Bank of Japan policy actions have a greater impact on Japanese government bond yields and the USD/JPY exchange rate than Federal Reserve actions.

Specifically, I would consider the hypothesis unsupported if:

- Japanese government bond yields react more strongly to Bank of Japan announcements than to Federal Reserve announcements.

- Changes in Bank of Japan policy rates explain movements in Japanese bond yields better than changes in U.S. Treasury yields.

- USD/JPY exchange rate movements are more closely correlated with Bank of Japan policy actions than with Federal Reserve actions or U.S.-Japan interest-rate differentials.

- Major Federal Reserve policy announcements produce little or no observable impact on Japanese government bond yields or the yen.

- Evidence indicates that Japanese financial markets have become increasingly decoupled from U.S. monetary policy following the Bank of Japan's recent policy normalization.

If these conditions are observed, I would conclude that the Bank of Japan has regained significant control over domestic financial conditions and that my hypothesis overstates the influence of Federal Reserve policy on Japanese markets.
